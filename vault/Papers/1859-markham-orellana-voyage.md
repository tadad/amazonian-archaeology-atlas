# The Voyage of Francisco de Orellana

## Notes

Open English edition used to map what the expedition narrative says. The compilation's printed chronology and names do not always match later critical editions; it is evidence for the transmitted account, not independent verification of its claims. A Spanish Carvajal edition is catalogued by the [FLACSO Andes repository](https://repositorio.flacsoandes.edu.ec/items/80e83afc-aeb7-4cc5-a563-4a1520a3341d).

## Page 102

The narrative begins after the party separates from Gonzalo Pizarro and says the current and distance prevented a return. It describes the onward voyage toward the ocean.

The barque strikes floating timber and has to be beached and repaired. The party then goes without settlements or provisions and reports eating leather and herbs.

## Page 103

The expedition reaches a defended settlement, takes food, and then exchanges goods for provisions. The edition reports feather and gold ornaments among leaders and information about powerful inland rulers. Orellana proposes constructing another brigantine.

## Page 104

The account reports manufacturing more than 2,000 nails with improvised bellows and says seven companions had died from hunger. After departure it describes dangerous meeting waters at a large right-bank tributary, a long uninhabited stretch, and later friendly villages supplying food.

## Page 105

Aparia is named as a principal chief. The account reports questions about the Coniapuyara ("great lords," rendered as Amazons) and a list of twenty other lords. It also describes building a second brigantine during a 35-day stay, with cotton and pitch used in caulking.

## Page 106

After leaving Aparia, the expedition reaches the densely settled province called Machiparo in the edition. The text associates its ruler with the name Aomagua and describes an encounter with many war canoes.

## Page 107

The narrative continues with a running battle, damp gunpowder, shields described as caiman or tapir hide, a food seizure, and multiple Spanish wounds.

## Page 108

Pedro de Ampudia is said to have died of his wounds. The account gives very large figures for shore and canoe forces, reports two days and nights of fighting, and describes the killing of an Indigenous commander. Those numbers are claims in the narrative, not archaeological population estimates.

## Page 109

After Machiparo the text names a River of Trinity with inhabited islands and describes a village with elaborately painted ceramics, a country house, palm-wood figures, and claims of gold and silver. No exact modern identification is established here.

## Page 110

The expedition passes a major black-water tributary. Markham's note identifies it as the Rio Negro; the location is useful as a modern hydrological anchor, while the editorial identification remains distinct from Carvajal's wording.

The same passage describes roads inland, a populous and fertile territory called Paguana in the edition, and large villages.

## Page 111

The narrative reports populous country, a house containing colored feather garments, and a region it calls the Province of the Gibbets. Seven structures displaying heads, roads said to be paved, and fruit trees are described before another fight.

## Page 112

A captive woman relays hearsay about white people and two white women inland. The text also describes stored drink, cotton cloth, a structure interpreted as a temple, villages on the left bank, strong current, and very large villages near a river bend.

## Page 113

The narrative reports women fighting at the head of an Indigenous force. The edition itself leaves the story's credibility to the reader, and Markham's note places the encounter near the Trombetas mouth.

## Page 114

The narrative reports another village ambush, an arrow wound that cost Carvajal sight in one eye, extremely closely spaced villages, and a long province called São João. It also describes many piraguas around large fertile islands.

## Page 115

A captive's reported description includes stone houses, walled cities, precious metal, and powerful female rulers. The narrative explicitly says that these details could not be affirmed; the atlas does not map the hearsay cities.

The edition then places the expedition in a territory called Caripuna and repeats reports of rich settlements and silver.

## Page 116

The narrative describes poisoned arrows, the death of Antonio de Carranca, and the party's first observation of an ebb tide. It includes Carvajal's marvelous story of a bird that allegedly followed the vessels and called near settlements. Later fighting among inhabited tidal islands is said to have caused García de Soria's death, after which the text names Chipayo territory.

## Page 117

The account describes many right-bank villages, low terrain, inhabited islands, and tides. One brigantine strikes a snag and floods during an attack; the expedition later spends eighteen days repairing the vessels and making nails.

## Page 118

The party reportedly makes rigging from grass and sails from blankets, survives for two weeks on shellfish, and proceeds without a pilot or compass. The expedition exits the river between estuarine islands and enters the Atlantic. This supports a broad route endpoint, not an exact channel reconstruction.
